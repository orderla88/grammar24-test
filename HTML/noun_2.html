Common/Proper
  • Common noun names general, unspecific object of its class. It is capitalized only at the start of a written sentence.

    Hurricane Catrina is one of the deadliest hurricanes in US history.

  • Proper noun names particular, specific object. It is always capitalized in writing.

    One of Mike Tyson's famous nicknames was an "Iron Mike".

Common Proper
cat Tom
country USA
man Michael
mountain Everest
mouse Jerry

Countable/Uncountable

  • Countable noun is counted by piece, i.e. one by one. It can be single or plural.

    There is a pencil on the table.
    I need some pencils for work.

    The plural form is usually made by adding siffixes -s/-es. Although, there are some exceptions.

    dog – dogs; beach – beaches;
    child – children man – men;

  • Uncountable noun is not counted by piece. It is considered single and is not pluralized. We always use singular verb after it.

    Money doesn't smell. (moneys)
    That is too much sugar. (sugars)

    We can put in front of them some countable phrases for counting:

    a piece of advice, a bowl of rice,
    a lesson of history, a glass of water;

    Common examples of uncountable nouns:

    • Liquids/Materials: water, milk, oil, petrol, metal, wood, paper;
    • Powders/Grains: rice, flour, sand, sugar, salt;
    • Abstract Ideas: advice, information, knowledge, fear, love, motivation;
    • Group Nouns: furniture, baggage/luggage, equipment, traffic, machinery
    • Natural Phenomena: weather, sunshine, rain, snow, lightning
    • Languages/Subjects: English, Spanish, Physics, History;

In general, we use:

  • any, some, lot(s) of with both countable/uncountable nouns;
  • many with countable, and much with uncountable nouns;
Countable Uncountable
We have a lot of opportunities. The baggage takes up a lot of space.
There are lots of cafes around. He earned lots of money in trade.
Do you have any questions? They don't have any freedom there.
Mom baked some cookies for us. Give me some time, please.
The tree had many apples on it. How much sugar do you want?

Possessive Form

Possessive form of noun indicates that something belongs or relates to it. In the sentence, we also say that noun is in possessive case. To make it, we generally add to the end of noun either an apostrophe with suffix -s, or only apostrophe. Here are the rules:

  • to single noun add ’s, even if it already ends in -s;

    Richard's car horse's tail
    Thomas's house boss's chair 1

  • if plural noun ends in -s, add only apostrophe;
    otherwise add ’s;

    boys' clothes calves' milk
    children’s room sheep's grass

  • if something belongs to several people, add ’s or ’ only after the last one;

    Katy and Mike’s dad
    Amy and Chris's turn

  • posessive forms can be chained;
    to replace chaining, use preposition of;

    John’s cousine’s children. (chaining)
    Children of John’s cousine.

1 In this case pronunciation would be [-iz].

boss's [bɔ:siz] chair
James's [dʒeimsiz] book.

Noun Phrase

Noun phrase includes a noun and its modifier(s). Types of noun modifiers:

  • Pre-modifiers come before the noun. They include articles, adjectives, numerals.
  • Post-modifiers come after the noun. They include phrases and clauses.
Modifier Noun phrase
Article a table, an apple, the Sun;
Adjective wooden table, green apple,
Pronoun my table, this apple,
Numeral second table, two apples;
Phrase table for two, apple to eat;
Clause table I've booked, apple I ate;

In the sentence, noun modifier serves as a determiner
